Abstract

Microstructured solids exhibit rich dispersive phenomena when acoustic wavelengths approach characteristic internal length scales, where classical local elasticity breaks down. While prior investigations of strain-gradient metamaterials have largely been restricted to one-dimensional chains or two-dimensional media with isotropic length scales, the role of microstructural orientation as an independent design knob remains unaddressed. In this work, we present a two-dimensional Bloch–Floquet finite element framework for Mindlin Form-II strain-gradient elasticity incorporating an anisotropic characteristic-length tensor derived from the second moments of a rotated ellipsoidal averaging domain, alongside an independent micro-inertia parameter ℓi. A C1-conforming Bogner–Fox–Schmit (BFS) 32-degree-of-freedom rectangular element is formulated, wherein complex Bloch phase transformations are consistently applied across nodal displacements as well as first-order and mixed-second-order derivative degrees of freedom. The numerical implementation is verified against an eight-test internal consistency suite and mesh convergence across 42 to 322 elements, yielding an observed empirical least-squares convergence rate of p=4.17 (fitted over the refinement levels admissible under a pre-declared measurement-resolution rule; 95% CI: [3.15,5.20]; no theoretical order claimed) and a locked operational numerical resolution floor of εΔ=4.63×10−11. In a homogeneous microstructured medium (Case H), we demonstrate that microstructural anisotropy induces strong directional frequency migration along Γ–X while strictly preserving the absence of complete omnidirectional band gaps (Δcomplete≤−0.3235). The resulting (θ,AR) design map reveals an orientation sensitivity metric Sθ that is largest at the most elongated configuration (2.34rad−1 at AR=10), accompanied by an acoustic wave-vector steering deviation of up to δmax=2.78∘ at k‾=0.5 (invariant to within 10−5deg over the full orientation grid θ∈[0∘,90∘] at AR=10, with the steering figure of merit Ms=ϕ∗(90∘)−ϕ∗(0∘) equal to 0∘ at AR=5 and 10∘ at AR=10 on the locked 5∘ sampling grid). Finally, asymptotic analysis and numerical evidence demonstrate that micro-inertia is constitutively necessary to bound high-wavenumber phase velocities to a finite horizon vT,∞=0.3162, ensuring physical admissibility.

1 Introduction

1.1 Microstructured Solids and Length-Scale Effects

The propagation of elastic waves through structured and architected solids has emerged as a cornerstone of modern mechanics, acoustic metamaterial design, and vibration isolation [1]. In classical Cauchy elasticity, stress is assumed to depend strictly on the local strain tensor, implying an inherently scale-free continuum without an internal characteristic length. However, when the acoustic or elastic wavelength approaches the characteristic dimensions of the underlying microstructural architecture—such as the grain size in textured polycrystals, the fiber spacing in advanced composites, or the cell pitch in additively manufactured lattices—classical continuum mechanics ceases to describe observed dispersive and size-dependent behaviors [2, 3]. Under such short-wave conditions, non-local strain interactions and higher-order deformation gradients become dominant, necessitating generalized continuum theories.

1.2 Gradient Elasticity and the Necessity of Micro-Inertia

Among higher-order continuum representations, Mindlin’s strain-gradient theory [2, 4] provides a rigorous variational framework incorporating displacement gradients into the strain energy density. In Mindlin’s Form-II formulation, the strain energy is parameterized by classical Lamé constants alongside higher-order characteristic length scales that penalize strain gradients [5, 6]. While static strain-gradient elasticity successfully captures boundary-layer stiffening, fracture regularisation, and micro-scale size effects, dynamic formulations encounter fundamental physical requirements. Specifically, if higher-order gradient elasticity is formulated without higher-order kinetic energy (i.e., with classical kinetic energy alone), the resulting dispersion relation yields phase and group velocities that grow unbounded as wavenumber k→∞ (vp∝k). As proven asymptotically in Appendix A, such unbounded high-wavenumber phase velocities violate causality and lead to physically inadmissible short-wave responses.

To overcome this pathology, Mindlin introduced the concept of micro-inertia, which supplements the classical kinetic energy density with terms proportional to the velocity gradients [2]. The micro-inertia parameter, denoted here by ℓi, acts as a dynamic length scale that balances the higher-order spatial stiffness operators. As established in 1D analyses by Papargyri-Beskou & Beskos [7] and Li et al. [8], non-zero micro-inertia bounds the high-wavenumber phase velocity to a finite acoustic limit, ensuring a physically admissible continuum representation across the entire spectrum.

1.3 Phononic Crystals and Direction-Dependent Band Gaps

In periodic elastodynamic media, spatial modulation of constitutive properties leads to band structures characterized by passing pass bands and destructive interference stop bands (band gaps) governed by the Floquet–Bloch theorem [1]. In classical composites, band gaps arise predominantly through periodic impedance contrast (Bragg scattering) or localized resonances. In contrast, higher-order gradient media exhibit intrinsic microstructural dispersion even in the absence of material heterogeneity. Furthermore, when microstructural length scales exhibit directional preference—such as elongated grains from rolling or oriented micro-fibers—the effective non-local interactions become anisotropic [9].

1.4 Literature Positioning and State of the Art

A comprehensive survey of published wave-dispersion and band-gap investigations based on gradient elasticity reveals that the literature has been overwhelmingly confined to one-dimensional systems or two-dimensional media with isotropic characteristic lengths. Table 1 provides a systematic positioning of the present study relative to foundational and recent contributions.

As shown in Table 1, early 1D analytical and transfer-matrix studies by Zheng & Wei [10], Papargyri-Beskou & Beskos [7], and Li, Wei & Zhou [8] explored basic microstructural dispersion and partial gaps in layered media. In 2D media, Zhan & Wei [9] investigated anisotropic band gaps within classical elasticity, while Hosseini & Zhang [11] developed a meshless radial point interpolation method for 2D gradient-elastic phononic crystals under the strict restriction of isotropic length scales (l1=l2). Recent advanced formulations have integrated multiphysics couplings in 1D layered media, including gradient thermoelasticity by Li et al. To the best of our knowledge, within the scope of published literature, no existing investigation provides a fully two-dimensional Bloch–Floquet finite element formulation that simultaneously incorporates an anisotropic characteristic length tensor, micro-inertia, and arbitrary microstructural orientation θ as a continuous design variable.

1.5 The Research Gap

From the literature positioning, three specific gaps are identified:

  1. (a) Published gradient-elastic elastodynamic studies incorporating micro-inertia are predominantly 1D.
  2. (b) Existing 2D gradient-elastic band structure investigations enforce isotropic characteristic lengths (l1=l2), thereby omitting directional orientation effects.
  3. (c) Microstructural orientation θ has not been quantitatively evaluated as an independent design variable for directional band filtering, iso-frequency contour tuning, and wave steering.

2 Anisotropic Strain-Gradient Continuum with Micro-Inertia

2.1 Ellipsoidal Nonlocal Averaging Domain and Length Tensor

In standard isotropic gradient elasticity, the microstructural influence is encapsulated by a single scalar length scale l. To model materials with oriented or directional microstructures, we consider a representative nonlocal averaging neighborhood ℰ defined as an ellipse in two dimensions with principal semi-axes l1 and l2:

ℰ0={(x1′,x2′)∈ℝ2|(x1′)2l12+(x2′)2l22≤1},(1)

where (x1′,x2′) denote local principal coordinates aligned with the microstructural symmetry axes. The second-moment characteristic-length tensor 𝐋0 associated with ℰ0 is diagonal in the principal frame:

𝐋0=[l1200l22].(2)

To preserve total averaging volume across microstructural distortions, we adopt a volume-equivalent (area-preserving) scaling rule referenced to an isotropic baseline length scale liso:

l1=lisoAR,l2=lisoAR,det⁡(𝐋0)=l12l22=liso4=const,(3)

where AR=l1/l2≥1 denotes the microstructural aspect ratio.

2.2 Orientation and Passive Tensor Rotation

When the microstructural principal axes are rotated by an orientation angle θ relative to the global Cartesian coordinate frame (x1,x2), the relationship between global coordinates 𝒙 and local principal coordinates 𝒙′ is given by the planar rotation matrix 𝑹(θ):

𝒙′=𝑹(θ)𝒙,𝑹(θ)=[cos⁡θsin⁡θ−sin⁡θcos⁡θ].(4)

Under passive coordinate transformation, the components of the characteristic-length tensor 𝐋(θ) in the global coordinate frame are given by:

𝐋(θ)=𝑹(θ)𝖳𝐋0𝑹(θ)=[cos⁡θ−sin⁡θsin⁡θcos⁡θ][l1200l22][cos⁡θsin⁡θ−sin⁡θcos⁡θ].(5)

Carrying out the matrix multiplication yields the explicit tensor components:

L11(θ)=l12cos2⁡θ+l22sin2⁡θ,(6)
L22(θ)=l12sin2⁡θ+l22cos2⁡θ,(7)
L12(θ)=L21(θ)=(l22−l12)cos⁡θsin⁡θ.(8)

Notice that for an elongated microstructure (l1>l2), the off-diagonal coupling term L12(θ) is strictly negative for 0<θ<90∘, reaching its extremum (l22−l12)/2 at θ=45∘. Furthermore, because 𝑹(θ) is an orthogonal transformation (𝑹𝖳𝑹=𝐈), the eigenvalues of 𝐋(θ) are strictly invariant under rotation:

spec(𝐋(θ))≡{l12,l22}∀θ∈[0,2π).(9)

Consequently, positive definiteness 𝒗𝖳𝐋(θ)𝒗>0 for all non-zero vectors 𝒗∈ℝ2 is guaranteed independently of the orientation angle θ.

2.3 Kinematics and Higher-Order Deformation Gradients

Assuming infinitesimal deformations under two-dimensional plane-strain conditions (u3=0,∂(⋅)/∂x3=0), the classical displacement gradient and infinitesimal strain tensor 𝜺 are:

εij=12(ui,j+uj,i),i,j∈{1,2}.(10)

In Mindlin’s strain-gradient theory, the kinematic state includes the second displacement gradient or strain-gradient third-order tensor 𝜼:

ηijk=εij,k=12(ui,jk+uj,ik),ηijk=ηjik.(11)

In 2D plane strain, the symmetric strain tensor 𝜺 contains 3 independent components (ε11,ε22,2ε12), while the strain-gradient tensor 𝜼 possesses 6 independent components (η111,η112,η221,η222,2η121,2η122).

2.4 Mindlin Form-II Constitutive Relations

We formulate the constitutive relations in Mindlin’s Form II, wherein the strain energy density W is decomposed into classical and higher-order strain-gradient contributions:

W=12σijεij+12τijkηijk,(12)

where 𝝈 is the Cauchy stress tensor and 𝝉 is the third-order double-stress (or dipolar stress) tensor. For an isotropic base material parameterized by Lamé constants λ and µ, the Cauchy stress satisfies Hooke’s law:

σij=Cijklεkl=λδijεkk+2µεij.(13)

The double-stress tensor τijk is conjugated to ηijk via an anisotropic gradient elasticity relation mediated by the characteristic-length tensor 𝐋:

τijk=110LklCijmnηmnl=110Lkl(λδijηmml+2µηijl),(14)

where the prefactor 1/10 arises from the second moment of the representative three-dimensional ellipsoidal averaging domain. Substituting Eq. (14) into Eq. (12) demonstrates that W is strictly non-negative and quadratic in both strains and strain gradients.

2.5 Micro-Inertia and Kinetic Energy Density

To formulate a causal, physically admissible dynamic theory, the kinetic energy density T is extended to include gradient inertia (micro-inertia):

T=12ρu˙iu˙i+12ρℓi2u˙i,ju˙i,j,(15)

where ρ is the macroscopic mass density and ℓi>0 is the independent micro-inertia length parameter. Applying Hamilton’s principle δ∫t1t2(T−W)dt=0 yields the strong-form equations of dynamic equilibrium in the bulk:

σij,j−τijk,jk=ρ(u¨i−ℓi2u¨i,jj),in Ω.(16)

The Laplacian term −ρℓi2u¨i,jj on the right-hand side represents micro-inertial resistance to localized velocity gradients. As shown in Section 7 and Appendix A, this dynamic term regularizes high-frequency dispersion and prevents phase velocity divergence.

2.6 Limit Ladder and Reductions

The formulated anisotropic strain-gradient model contains four well-defined physical limits:

  1. (i) Classical Elasticity Limit (lm→0,ℓi→0): Double stresses vanish (τijk→0), micro-inertia vanishes, and the classical Navier equations of motion σij,j=ρu¨i are recovered with dispersionless acoustic speeds cT=µ/ρ and cL=(λ+2µ)/ρ.
  2. (ii) Isotropic Gradient Elasticity (AR=1): Here l1=l2=liso, so L11=L22=liso2 and L12=0 for all θ, recovering isotropic Form-II gradient elasticity.
  3. (iii) Gradient Elasticity without Micro-Inertia (ℓi=0): Higher-order stiffness double stresses persist while dynamic regularisation is removed, producing an unbounded phase velocity branch (vp∝k→∞).
  4. (iv) Diagonal Rotation-Swap Symmetry (θ→θ+90∘ with l1↔l2): The components of 𝐋 satisfy L11(θ+90∘,l1,l2)=L22(θ,l2,l1) and L12(θ+90∘,l1,l2)=−L12(θ,l2,l1), establishing rigorous rotational symmetry.

2.7 Boundary Operators

The variational formulation on boundary Γ=∂Ω with unit outward normal 𝒏 yields natural and essential boundary conditions:

pi=σijnj−njnkτijk,k,Ri=njnkτijk,(17)

where pi is the effective surface traction and Ri is the higher-order double traction. The corresponding work-conjugate kinematic variables are the displacement ui and the normal displacement derivative ∂ui/∂n≡ui,jnj.

3 Bloch–Floquet Formulation for C1 Media

3.1 Periodic Lattice and Brillouin Zone

We consider a two-dimensional infinite periodic medium constructed by tiling a square unit cell Ωcell=[0,L]×[0,L] with lattice vectors:

𝒂1=[L0],𝒂2=[0L].(18)

The reciprocal lattice vectors 𝒃1,𝒃2 satisfying the orthogonality relation 𝒂i⋅𝒃j=2πδij span the reciprocal space:

𝒃1=2πL[10],𝒃2=2πL[01].(19)

The first Brillouin zone (BZ) corresponds to the symmetric square domain ℬ=[−π/L,π/L]×[−π/L,π/L]. For an isotropic square lattice (AR=1), the point-group symmetry is C4v (p4mm), allowing reduction to the triangular irreducible Brillouin zone (IBZ) bounded by the high-symmetry path Γ(0,0)→X(π/L,0)→M(π/L,π/L)→Γ(0,0). When anisotropic microstructural orientation (AR>1) is introduced, the spatial symmetry is reduced to C2v (for θ∈{0∘,90∘}) or C2 (for general θ), under which the irreducible domain expands to the half-BZ [0,π/L]×[−π/L,π/L].

3.2 Bloch Theorem on C1 Degrees of Freedom

Under the time-harmonic assumption 𝒖(𝒙,t)=𝒖~(𝒙)ei(𝒌⋅𝒙−ωt), Floquet–Bloch theory states that the displacement field satisfies:

𝒖(𝒙+𝒂α)=𝒖(𝒙)ei𝒌⋅𝒂α,α∈{1,2}.(20)

A distinctive feature of fourth-order gradient elasticity discretizations is the presence of first- and second-order spatial derivatives as independent nodal degrees of freedom in the conforming C1 finite element space. Differentiating the Bloch relation Eq. (20) with respect to spatial coordinates xβ yields:

∂𝒖∂xβ(𝒙+𝒂α)=∂𝒖∂xβ(𝒙)ei𝒌⋅𝒂α,∂2𝒖∂x1∂x2(𝒙+𝒂α)=∂2𝒖∂x1∂x2(𝒙)ei𝒌⋅𝒂α.(21)

Equations (20) and (21) demonstrate that all derivative degrees of freedom transform with the exact same scalar Bloch phase factor ei𝒌⋅𝒂α as the primary displacement field. Applying distinct or inconsistent phases to gradient DOFs would violate the continuous differentiability of the Bloch eigenfunctions across unit cell boundaries.

3.3 Non-Dimensionalisation

To ensure scale-independent results, all physical variables are non-dimensionalized using the unit cell length L, the base shear modulus µ, and base density ρ:

k‾=kLπ,ω‾=ωω0,ω0=µρL2,l‾m=lmL,ℓ‾i=ℓiL,v‾p=ω/kµ/ρ.(22)

With these definitions, classical shear wave speed is non-dimensionalized to cT=1.0 and longitudinal wave speed to cL=(λ+2µ)/µ=3≈1.732 (for ν=0.25,λ/µ=1.0).

3.4 Band-Gap Hierarchy and Observables

For every Bloch wave vector 𝒌, solving the reduced unit-cell eigenproblem yields a discrete set of eigenfrequencies ω‾n(𝒌), ordered as ω‾1(𝒌)≤ω‾2(𝒌)≤⋯≤ω‾N(𝒌). To avoid ambiguity, band gaps are categorized according to the project’s strict three-level hierarchy:

  1. (a) Leg Gap Δ[leg]: The spectral separation between bands n and n+1 along an individual path segment (e.g., ΔGX=min𝒌∈Γ−X⁡ω‾n+1(𝒌)−max𝒌∈Γ−X⁡ω‾n(𝒌)).
  2. (b) Path Gap Δ[path]: The gap evaluated over the complete closed contour Γ–X–M–Γ:
    Δpath=min𝒌∈path⁡ω‾n+1(𝒌)−max𝒌∈path⁡ω‾n(𝒌).(23)
  3. (c) Complete (Omnidirectional) Gap Δ[complete]: The gap evaluated over the entire two-dimensional Brillouin zone ℬ:
    Δcomplete=min𝒌∈ℬ⁡ω‾n+1(𝒌)−max𝒌∈ℬ⁡ω‾n(𝒌).(24)

By set inclusion, these gap metrics satisfy the strict mathematical inequality:

Δ[leg]≥Δ[path]≥Δ[complete].(25)

A positive leg gap (ΔGX>0) designates a directional stop band along that propagation direction, but must not be termed a complete band gap unless Δcomplete>0.

To quantify the tunability of directional gaps with respect to microstructural rotation, we define the orientation sensitivity metric Sθ:

Sθ=maxθ⁡|∂Δ∂θ|maxθ⁡Δ[rad−1].(26)

The group velocity vector 𝒗g represents the velocity of energy transport and is computed as the gradient of the dispersion surface:

𝒗g=∇𝒌ω.(27)

The steering deviation angle δ measures the angular deviation of energy propagation from the phase wave-vector direction:

δ=∠𝒗g−∠𝒌.(28)

4 C1 Finite Element Discretisation

4.1 Bogner–Fox–Schmit Bicubic Hermite Element

In Mindlin Form-II gradient elasticity, the weak form of equilibrium requires square-integrable second derivatives of displacement, placing the variational space in the Sobolev space H2(Ω). Consequently, standard C0-continuous Lagrange elements are non-conforming and suffer from severe convergence pathologies. To guarantee conforming C1 inter-element continuity across rectangular element boundaries, we adopt the Bogner–Fox–Schmit (BFS) bicubic element [15].

For a rectangular element of dimensions 2a×2b, the displacement components u1(x,y) and u2(x,y) are interpolated independently as tensor products of one-dimensional cubic Hermite polynomials:

ui(x,y)=∑a=14∑p=14Na,p(x,y)di,a,pe,i∈{1,2},(29)

where a∈{1,2,3,4} indexes the four element vertices, and p∈{1,2,3,4} denotes the four nodal degrees of freedom per displacement component:

𝒅i,ae=[ui∂ui∂x∂ui∂y∂2ui∂x∂y]at node a𝖳.(30)

With 4 vertices and 2 displacement components, each BFS element contains 4×2×4=32 degrees of freedom. Figure 3 illustrates the nodal DOF arrangement and the complex phase mapping on periodic boundaries. All band-structure and design-map results reported below are computed with a uniform mesh of n×n=16×16 BFS elements per unit cell (h=L/n=0.0625, hence 8n2=2048 degrees of freedom per cell); the element order n is listed in Table 5.

4.2 Element Stiffness Matrices

The strain tensor 𝜺 and strain-gradient tensor 𝜼 within element e are related to the element nodal displacement vector 𝒅e∈ℝ32 via kinematic strain-displacement matrices 𝑩c and 𝑩kg:

𝜺=𝑩c𝒅e,𝜼k≡∂𝜺∂xk=𝑩kg𝒅e,k∈{1,2}.(31)

The total element stiffness matrix 𝑲e separates cleanly into classical and gradient contributions:

𝑲e=𝑲c+𝑲g(θ,AR),(32)

where the classical stiffness matrix is:

𝑲c=∫Ωe(𝑩c)𝖳𝑪𝑩cdΩ,(33)

and the anisotropic gradient stiffness matrix is:

𝑲g(θ,AR)=110∑k=12∑l=12Lkl(θ)∫Ωe(𝑩kg)𝖳𝑪𝑩lgdΩ.(34)

Because the shape functions are bicubic polynomials, the element-matrix integrands are products of their derivatives and reach degree six per coordinate; a standard 4×4 Gauss–Legendre rule (exact through degree seven per axis) is therefore the minimal tensor rule that integrates all homogeneous-element terms exactly without aliasing or rank deficiency. In elements cut by the inclusion, the discontinuous material field is instead integrated by sub-cell Gauss sampling with an O(h) geometric approximation (Section 6.2).

4.3 Element Mass and Micro-Inertia Matrices

Similarly, the kinetic energy yields classical and micro-inertia mass contributions:

𝑴e=𝑴0+ℓi2𝑴g,(35)

with:

𝑴0=ρ∫Ωe𝑵𝖳𝑵dΩ,𝑴g=ρ∑k=12∫Ωe(∂𝑵∂xk)𝖳(∂𝑵∂xk)dΩ.(36)

Both 𝑴0 and 𝑴g are symmetric and positive semi-definite; their sum 𝑴e is strictly positive definite for any positive density ρ>0.

4.4 Bloch-Periodic Master–Slave Elimination

To apply the Bloch boundary conditions across the unit cell boundary ∂Ωcell, the global degrees of freedom 𝒅 are partitioned into interior (I), master boundaries (M), and slave boundaries (S):

𝒅=[𝒅I𝒅M𝒅S].(37)

The slave degrees of freedom on the top and right boundaries are related to the corresponding master degrees of freedom on the bottom and left boundaries via the Bloch transformation:

𝒅S=𝑻SM(𝒌)𝒅M,(38)

where 𝑻SM(𝒌) is a diagonal complex phase matrix formed by evaluating eikxL, eikyL, or ei(kx+ky)L for each periodic node pair. As derived in Section 3, identical phase factors apply to the displacement, first-order, and cross-derivative DOFs. Defining the master-slave transformation matrix 𝑻(𝒌):

𝒅=𝑻(𝒌)𝒅‾,𝑻(𝒌)=[𝐈II𝟎𝟎𝐈MM𝟎𝑻SM(𝒌)],𝒅‾=[𝒅I𝒅M],(39)

the reduced stiffness and mass matrices are obtained through Galerkin projection:

𝑲‾(𝒌)=𝑻(𝒌)𝖧𝑲𝑻(𝒌),𝑴‾(𝒌)=𝑻(𝒌)𝖧𝑴𝑻(𝒌).(40)

4.5 Reduced Hermitian Generalized Eigenproblem

The dispersion curves ω‾n(𝒌) are determined by solving the reduced generalized eigenvalue problem:

[𝑲‾(𝒌)−ω2𝑴‾(𝒌)]𝒅‾=𝟎.(41)

Because the global matrices 𝑲 and 𝑴 are real symmetric and 𝑻(𝒌) is unitary on the boundary blocks, the reduced operators are strictly Hermitian:

𝑲‾(𝒌)𝖧=𝑲‾(𝒌),𝑴‾(𝒌)𝖧=𝑴‾(𝒌)>0,(42)

guaranteeing real eigenvalues ωn2≥0. Furthermore, shifting 𝒌 by a reciprocal lattice vector 𝑮 yields 𝑲‾(𝒌+𝑮)=𝑲‾(𝒌), ensuring exact periodicity across reciprocal space.

4.6 Modal Assurance Criterion and Branch Tracking

Along high-symmetry path segments, band crossing occurs between modes of differing spatial symmetry. To maintain physical branch continuity and resolve mode identity, we employ the Modal Assurance Criterion (MAC) between eigenvectors evaluated at adjacent wave vectors 𝒌1 and 𝒌2:

MACmn=|𝒅‾m(𝒌1)𝖧𝑴‾𝒅‾n(𝒌2)|2(𝒅‾m(𝒌1)𝖧𝑴‾𝒅‾m(𝒌1))(𝒅‾n(𝒌2)𝖧𝑴‾𝒅‾n(𝒌2)).(43)

When the off-diagonal MAC indicates branch exchange, step-halving continuation is triggered. However, in accordance with standard phononic band-structure reporting, band gaps are formally evaluated using frequency-ordered eigenvalues ω‾1(𝒌)≤ω‾2(𝒌)≤⋯≤ω‾N(𝒌), avoiding subjective mode tracking ambiguities.

5 Verification and Numerical Consistency Framework

5.1 Multi-Layer Verification and Validation Strategy

To establish uncompromising scientific credibility, this investigation structures code and model verification across a hierarchical five-layer verification and validation framework:

6 Band Structure and Directional Filtering Results

6.1 Microstructure-Induced Dispersion in Homogeneous Media (Case H)

We first investigate elastodynamic dispersion in a geometrically homogeneous unit cell with anisotropic strain-gradient microstructure (Case H). In classical Cauchy elasticity, a homogeneous square unit cell exhibits trivial folded acoustic branches without band gaps or dispersion. In sharp contrast, higher-order strain-gradient stiffness and micro-inertia induce intrinsic dispersion.

Figure 6 compares the band structures for an isotropic microstructure (AR=1) against an anisotropic microstructure (AR=10) at orientations θ=0∘ and θ=45∘. In all cases, two non-dispersive acoustic branches emerge from the Γ point with zero frequency (ω‾1,2(0)=0), corresponding to rigid body translations. As wavenumber increases along Γ–X, higher-order gradient effects become pronounced. For the isotropic case (AR=1), modes at the X point exhibit four-fold point-group degeneracy. When microstructural anisotropy is introduced at an angle (AR=10,θ=45∘), this degeneracy is lifted, shifting acoustic branch frequencies and opening a directional stop band ΔGX=+0.0043 between bands 2 and 3 along the Γ–X path.

We emphasize that this stop band is strictly directional: along the X–M and M–Γ directions, other transmission branches overlap this frequency interval. Evaluating the omnidirectional minimum and maximum across the entire Brillouin zone confirms Δcomplete≤−0.3235, verifying that no complete omnidirectional band gap exists in a homogeneous medium.

6.2 Periodic Phononic Inclusions (Case C)

While the homogeneous anisotropic medium (Case H) produces strictly directional stop bands (Δcomplete≤−0.3235), introducing periodic material heterogeneity in the form of high-contrast inclusions embedded in a host matrix (Case C / Study S2) enables the opening of broad, complete omnidirectional band gaps. Following the peer-reviewed phononic crystal literature [9], we consider a two-dimensional square lattice of pitch L composed of an epoxy matrix (ρm=1142kg/m3,µm=1.48GPa,λm=4.57GPa) reinforced by a central circular YBCO ceramic inclusion of radius r0=0.30L (ρi=6333kg/m3,µi=37.0GPa,λi=95.0GPa). These elastic and inertial constants are source-derived from Zhan & Wei (2010) Table 1 [9] (provenance tag [A] in Table 5). The high shear modulus contrast (χµ=µi/µm=25.0) and mass density contrast (χρ=ρi/ρm=5.546), combined with gradient length scales ℓm=0.10L and ℓi=0.20L and inclusion radius ratio r0/L=0.30 (study design choices tagged [S]), provide strong acoustic impedance mismatch. Nondimensionalization is scaled by the transverse acoustic shear speed of the matrix ct,m=µm/ρm=1138.4m/s and characteristic Bragg frequency ω0,C=πct,m/L, differing from Case H which directly sets non-dimensional baseline units µ=ρ=L=1.

To resolve the curved circular interface within C1-conforming Bogner–Fox–Schmit rectangular elements without violating inter-element conformality (TV18), we adopt the standard Cartesian immersed-domain approach with internal Gauss–Legendre quadrature [15]. At each Gauss integration point (xg,yg), the material indicator function χ(𝒙) evaluates whether the point lies within the inclusion (r≤r0) or the matrix (r>r0). This formulation strictly preserves the inter-element C1 continuity of the tensor-product bicubic Hermite shape functions without introducing boundary-fitted geometric distortion. We note that the circular boundary is approximated via piecewise numerical quadrature integration rather than an exact boundary-conforming polygonal representation.

The resulting Bloch band structure along the high-symmetry path Γ–X–M–Γ and its sensitivity to inclusion radius r0/a are displayed in Figure 7.

As shown in Figure 7(a), the strong impedance contrast between matrix and inclusion separates acoustic modes from high-frequency optical modes, opening a broad complete stop band between Band 3 and Band 4 over the normalized frequency range ω‾∈[4.5698,7.1430] (values at the 4×4 mesh, 21×21 zone grid). Two-dimensional Brillouin-zone grid scans directly evaluate the complete gap Δcomplete=min𝒌⁡ω‾4(𝒌)−max𝒌⁡ω‾3(𝒌): at the 4×4 mesh the gap is Δcomplete=2.5732 on 11×11 and 21×21 grids and 2.5728 on the finer 41×41 grid (the upper edge min⁡ω‾4=7.1430→7.1426 lies on the Γ–M diagonal and is approached as the grid refines), giving a complete gap width of Δcomplete=2.5732 (Δ/ω‾mid=43.94%) to a Brillouin-zone sampling accuracy of ∼4×10−4. The strict directional-to-complete subset inequality Δ[leg]≥Δ[path]≥Δ[complete] is satisfied without exception across all path segments (values at the 4×4 mesh):

min⁡(Δ[Γ--X],Δ[X--M],Δ[M--Γ])=min⁡(2.6114,2.7563,3.2871)=2.6114
≥Δ[path]=2.5732≥Δ[complete]=2.5732,(48)

7 Anisotropic Wave Steering and Physical Admissibility

7.1 Wave-Vector Steering and Group Velocity Deviation

To examine energy redirection, we analyze the propagation of elastic waves along a constant wavenumber contour |𝒌|=k‾=0.5 parameterized by propagation angle ϕ∈[0,2π). In an isotropic elastic medium, energy travels collinear with the wave vector (𝒗g∥𝒌), yielding zero angular deviation (δ=∠𝒗g−∠𝒌=0). When microstructural anisotropy is introduced, the group velocity deviates from the phase vector.

Figure 12(a) plots the steering deviation angle δ(ϕ) as a function of wave-vector angle ϕ for AR∈{1,5,10} at fixed microstructural orientation θ=45∘. For the isotropic medium (AR=1), the steering deviation vanishes (δmax=0.00∘, well within numerical noise). As the microstructural aspect ratio increases to AR=5 and AR=10, significant wave steering develops, with maximum deviation angles reaching δmax=1.41∘ and δmax=2.78∘, respectively. Concurrently, Figure 12(b) reveals that the group velocity magnitude |𝒗g|(ϕ) undergoes pronounced directional modulation, demonstrating that microstructural orientation acts as an active acoustic steering mechanism.

7.2 Orientation Sweep of the Steering Pattern and Figure of Merit

To quantify orientation control of the steering pattern, the constant-contour analysis is extended to the full orientation grid θ∈{0∘,15∘,30∘,45∘,60∘,75∘,90∘} at AR∈{5,10} on the same ring |𝒌|=k‾=0.5, using the identical acoustic branch, sampling, and central-difference group-velocity evaluation as above.

Table 7 and Figure 13 report the computed results. The deviation maximum is orientation independent across the full sweep to within 10−5deg: δmax=1.4146∘ at AR=5 and 2.7846∘ at AR=10 at every θ (relative variation below 5×10−4%). The deviation-maximum direction follows ϕ∗(θ)=(45∘−θ)mod⁡90∘ on the locked grid (with excursions of at most 5∘ at AR=10), i.e., the steering lobes continuously counter-rotate with the microstructure inside each 90∘ sector.

Defining the steering figure of merit

Ms(AR)=ϕ∗(90∘,AR)−ϕ∗(0∘,AR),(49)

the evaluation yields

Ms(AR=5)=0∘(ϕ∗(90∘)=ϕ∗(0∘)=45∘),Ms(AR=10)=10∘(ϕ∗(0∘)=40∘, ϕ∗(90∘)=50∘),(50)

The residual steering figure of merit is bounded by the 5∘ sampling step of the ring sweep, and its value is set by the two field symmetries verified numerically over the entire sweep (maximum residuals 1.3×10−6deg for both): the headless periodicity δ(ϕ+180∘;θ)=δ(ϕ;θ) and the mirror co-symmetry δ(ϕ;θ)=δ(90∘−ϕ;90∘−θ), the latter being the group-velocity counterpart of the rotation–swap symmetry of the length-scale tensor established in Section 2 (Eq. (5)). Because the θ=90∘ deviation field is the mirror of the θ=0∘ field, the unsigned principal steering axis (mod 90∘) of the rotated microstructure is the mirror image (mod 90∘) of its unrotated counterpart, and δmax is invariant under θ→90∘−θ to within 10−7deg. Orientation tuning therefore repositions the steering lobes within the fundamental sector—following (45∘−θ)mod⁡90∘—while the attainable deviation amplitude is invariant over all orientations to within 10−5deg (relative variation below 5×10−4%).

7.3 Energy Partition and Double-Stress Work

In Mindlin Form-II gradient elasticity, total strain energy W is composed of classical energy Wc=12∫ΩσijεijdΩ and gradient energy Wg=12∫ΩτijkηijkdΩ. Figure 14(a) plots the gradient energy partition fraction Wg/W as a function of non-dimensional wavenumber k‾ along the Γ–X direction.

At long wavelengths (k‾=0.1), gradient strain energy constitutes a negligible fraction (Wg/W=0.20%), indicating that classical elasticity dominates macroscopic deformation. However, as the wavelength approaches the unit cell dimensions (k‾→1.0), double-stress work increases monotonically, accounting for 16.49% of the total strain energy. This substantial energetic contribution underscores the necessity of higher-order gradient formulations in short-wave regimes.

7.4 Micro-Inertia and High-Wavenumber Admissibility

Figure 14(b) provides numerical confirmation of the asymptotic analysis derived in Appendix A. For the formulation lacking micro-inertia (ℓi=0), the computed phase velocity grows without bound with increasing wavenumber, reaching v‾p=39.75 at k‾=200. In sharp contrast, incorporating micro-inertia (ℓi>0) regularizes the high-frequency spectrum, causing the phase velocity to asymptote to a strictly finite horizon:

v‾T,∞=l‾eff10ℓ‾i=0.2010×0.20=110≈0.3162.(51)

At k‾=200, the finite element model yields v‾p=0.3163, in remarkable agreement with the theoretical horizon (relative difference 0.03%). This confirms the constitutive role of micro-inertia in ensuring bounded wave speeds and causality.

8 Discussion and Limitations

8.1 Physical Implications for Metamaterial Design

The results presented in Sections 6 and 7 establish that an anisotropic characteristic length tensor offers an effective mechanism for tuning directional wave propagation. In classical phononic crystals, directional filtering is typically achieved through geometric asymmetry of inclusions or complex multi-phase material architectures. Here, we demonstrate that in materials possessing intrinsic microstructural texture—such as cold-rolled alloys, oriented fibrous media, or architected sub-lattices—directional filtering can be manipulated solely by adjusting the orientation of the microstructural axes relative to the lattice coordinate frame.

8.2 Distinction Between Directional Stop Bands and Complete Band Gaps

A critical finding of this study is the strict adherence to the gap hierarchy Δ[leg]≥Δ[path]≥Δ[complete]. In the homogeneous Case H configuration, while substantial directional stop bands open along specific high-symmetry paths (e.g., ΔGX=0.0677 at AR=7,θ=0∘, and ΔGX=0.0043 at AR=10,θ=45∘), the global complete band gap remains strictly non-positive (Δcomplete≤−0.3235). Propagating modes in other directions (specifically along X–M and M–Γ) span the stop-band frequencies. In contrast, introducing periodic multi-phase impedance contrast (Case C) opens a broad complete omnidirectional band gap, directly evaluated over full two-dimensional Brillouin-zone grids as Δcomplete=2.5732 (Δ/ω‾mid=43.94%) at the 4×4 mesh. The gap remains open but decreases under mesh refinement (2.2504 at 8×8, 2.0722 at 16×16, 1.9736 at 32×32, 1.9179 at 64×64) and is not mesh-converged at the resolutions computed to date, so 2.5732 is quoted as the 4×4-mesh width rather than a converged continuum value.

  2. Status of Case C Phononic Crystal Study (Study S2): Case C is documented in Section 6.2. The circular inclusion geometry is treated with the locked immersed Gauss–Legendre quadrature of TV18 (sub-cell material sampling; the circular boundary is approximated by piecewise numerical quadrature rather than an exact boundary-conforming representation), preserving C1 conformality of the tensor-product BFS element without grid distortion. The complete band gap between Band 3 and Band 4 is demonstrated across 11×11 and 21×21 2D Brillouin zone grids, its directional subset inequality is mathematically confirmed, and the complete gap remains open under mesh refinement from 4×4 to 64×64 elements while its width decreases (2.5732→2.2504→2.0722→1.9736→1.9179; not mesh-converged).

Non-linear strain-gradient effects, thermal dissipation, and 3D out-of-plane polarization couplings are beyond the scope of this investigation.

9 Conclusions

In this investigation, a two-dimensional Bloch–Floquet finite element framework was developed for Mindlin Form-II strain-gradient elasticity incorporating an anisotropic characteristic-length tensor and micro-inertia. The principal conclusions are summarized as follows:

  1. Anisotropic Length-Scale Formulation: By deriving the characteristic length tensor 𝐋(θ) from the second moments of an ellipsoidal averaging domain, microstructural aspect ratio AR and orientation θ were successfully incorporated as continuous, independent design knobs. The positive definiteness of 𝐋(θ) was shown to be strictly invariant under spatial rotation.
  2. Consistent C1 Bloch Discretisation: A 32-degree-of-freedom Bogner–Fox–Schmit bicubic rectangular element was formulated. We demonstrated that conforming C1 representations require identical complex Bloch phase transformations across displacement, first-order, and cross-derivative nodal degrees of freedom, preserving the Hermiticity and periodicity of the reduced eigenproblem.
  3. Rigorous Numerical Verification: The finite element framework satisfied an eight-test internal consistency suite with zero violations. Monotone mesh convergence across 42 to 322 grids supports an observed least-squares rate of p=4.17 (fitted over the three refinement levels admissible under the pre-declared measurement-resolution rule; the 322 level is reported as resolution-limited and is not part of the fit) together with an operational numerical resolution floor of εΔ=4.63×10−11.
  4. Directional Band-Gap Filtering: In homogeneous media (Case H), microstructural anisotropy lifts high-symmetry mode degeneracies, producing directional stop bands up to ΔGX=0.0677 along Γ–X while strictly adhering to the absence of complete band gaps (Δcomplete≤−0.3235). The orientation sensitivity metric Sθ is largest at the most elongated configuration, 2.34rad−1 at AR=10.
  5. Complete Omnidirectional Band Gaps via Periodic Heterogeneity: Embedding high-contrast circular inclusions (Case C) opens a broad complete omnidirectional band gap (Δcomplete=2.5732, Δ/ω‾mid=43.94%), strictly obeying the directional-to-complete subset hierarchy Δ[leg]≥Δ[path]≥Δ[complete]. The gap remains open under mesh and quadrature refinements while its width decreases with mesh refinement (Δcomplete=2.5732, 2.2504, 2.0722, 1.9736, 1.9179 at 4×4, 8×8, 16×16, 32×32, 64×64; not mesh-converged), so Δcomplete=2.5732 is stated at the 4×4 mesh.
  6. Acoustic Wave Steering: At constant wavenumber |𝒌|=0.5, group velocity vectors deviate from phase vectors by up to δmax=2.78∘ at AR=10 (θ=45∘). A full orientation sweep (θ∈[0∘,90∘], AR∈{5,10}) yields δmax=1.4146∘ (AR=5) and 2.7846∘ (AR=10), invariant across the sweep to within 10−5deg, a principal steering axis tracking (45∘−θ)mod⁡90∘, exact headless and mirror co-symmetries of the deviation field (1.3×10−6deg residuals for both), and a steering figure of merit Ms=ϕ∗(90∘)−ϕ∗(0∘) of 0∘ (AR=5) and 10∘ (AR=10, two 5∘ sampling steps), demonstrating repositionable—yet exactly periodically symmetric—active acoustic steering without material contrast.
  7. Necessity of Micro-Inertia: Both analytical asymptotic derivations and numerical computations confirmed that omitting micro-inertia (ℓi=0) results in an unbounded phase velocity (vp∝k→∞), whereas ℓi>0 bounds the wave speed to a finite asymptotic horizon (vT,∞=0.3162), ensuring dynamic causality.

These findings provide a solid theoretical and computational foundation for designing architected metamaterials that leverage microstructural orientation for directional wave guiding and vibration suppression.

A High-Wavenumber Phase Velocity Asymptotics

This appendix provides the formal mathematical proof that strain-gradient elasticity without micro-inertia (ℓi=0) results in an unbounded phase velocity as wavenumber k→∞, whereas the inclusion of micro-inertia (ℓi>0) strictly bounds the wave speed to a finite asymptotic horizon.

Consider one-dimensional transverse shear wave propagation in a Mindlin Form-II gradient-elastic continuum governed by the equation of motion:

µ∂2u∂x2−110µleff2∂4u∂x4=ρ∂2u∂t2−ρℓi2∂4u∂x2∂t2,(52)

where µ is the shear modulus, ρ is the mass density, leff2=𝒌^⋅𝐋⋅𝒌^ is the effective characteristic gradient-stiffness length scale (with the factor 1/10 inherited from the second moment of the 3D ellipsoidal domain), and ℓi is the micro-inertia length parameter. Substituting the time-harmonic plane wave ansatz u(x,t)=Aei(kx−ωt) into Eq. (52) yields:

−µk2−110µleff2k4=−ρω2−ρℓi2ω2k2.(53)

Rearranging terms provides the exact analytical dispersion relation:

ω2(k)=µρk2(1+110leff2k2)1+ℓi2k2.(54)

The phase velocity vp(k)≡ω(k)/k is therefore:

vp(k)=µρ1+110leff2k21+ℓi2k2=cT1+110leff2k21+ℓi2k2,(55)

where cT=µ/ρ is the classical acoustic shear wave speed.

Case 1: Formulation without Micro-Inertia (ℓi=0).

Setting ℓi=0 in Eq. (55) yields:

vp(k)=cT1+110leff2k2.(56)

In the short-wave limit (k→∞), the phase velocity behaves asymptotically as:

limk→∞⁡vp(k)=limk→∞⁡cTleff10k→∞.(57)

Similarly, the group velocity vg(k)=dω/dk is given by:

vg(k)=cT1+210leff2k21+110leff2k2∼2cTleff10k→∞as k→∞.(58)

Consequently, higher-order stiffness without micro-inertia leads to unbounded propagation speeds at high frequencies, violating relativistic causality and physical admissibility.

Case 2: Formulation with Micro-Inertia (ℓi>0).

When ℓi>0, expanding Eq. (55) as k→∞ produces:

vp,∞≡limk→∞⁡vp(k)=cTlimk→∞⁡110leff2k2(1+10leff2k2)ℓi2k2(1+1ℓi2k2)=cTleff10ℓi.(59)

Non-dimensionalizing by cT=1.0 yields the finite asymptotic horizon:

v‾T,∞=l‾eff10ℓ‾i.(60)

For our baseline Case H parameters (leff=liso=0.20m, ℓi=0.20m from Table 5, with l‾eff=0.20 and ℓ‾i=0.20), the transverse asymptotic horizon is:

v‾T,∞=0.2010×0.20=110≈0.3162.(61)

As verified numerically in Section 7, the finite element phase velocity at k‾=200 attains v‾p=0.3163, matching the analytical horizon within 0.03%. Micro-inertia is thus a constitutive necessity that guarantees bounded wave speeds across all scales.

B Time-Averaged Energy Flux in Form-II Gradient Elasticity

In this appendix, we derive the conservation of elastodynamic energy and the explicit form of the time-averaged Poynting-type energy flux vector 𝑺 in a Mindlin Form-II gradient-elastic solid with micro-inertia.

The local balance of mechanical energy is expressed by the continuity relation:

∂∂t(W+T)+∇⋅𝑺=0,(62)

where W and T are the strain and kinetic energy densities defined in Eqs. (12) and (15). Differentiating W and T with respect to time gives:

∂W∂t=σijε˙ij+τijkη˙ijk=σiju˙i,j+τijku˙i,jk,(63)
∂T∂t=ρu˙iu¨i+ρℓi2u˙i,ju¨i,j.(64)

Expanding the spatial derivative terms in Eq. (63) using the product rule:

σiju˙i,j=(σiju˙i),j−σij,ju˙i,(65)
τijku˙i,jk=(τijku˙i,k),j−τijk,ju˙i,k=(τijku˙i,k),j−(τijk,ku˙i),j+τijk,jku˙i.(66)

Similarly, the micro-inertial rate term in Eq. (64) expands to:

ρℓi2u˙i,ju¨i,j=(ρℓi2u˙iu¨i,j),j−ρℓi2u˙iu¨i,jj.(67)

Adding Eqs. (63) and (64) and substituting the strong-form dynamic equation of motion σij,j−τijk,jk=ρ(u¨i−ℓi2u¨i,jj) from Eq. (16), the bulk terms identically cancel:

∂(W+T)∂t=[(σij−τijk,k)u˙i+τikju˙i,k+ρℓi2u˙iu¨i,j],j.(68)

Comparing Eq. (68) with Eq. (62) identifies the instantaneous energy flux vector Sj:

Sj=−(σij−τijk,k)u˙i−τikju˙i,k−ρℓi2u˙iu¨i,j.(69)

The three terms in Eq. (69) correspond physically to: (i) classical traction power modulated by double-stress divergence, (ii) higher-order double-stress power working on velocity gradients, and (iii) micro-inertial flux.

For time-harmonic fields represented in complex notation 𝒖(𝒙,t)=Re[𝒖~(𝒙)e−iωt], the cycle-averaged energy flux ⟨Sj⟩=1𝒯∫0𝒯Sjdt is given by:

⟨Sj⟩=−12Re[(σ~ij−τ~ijk,k)(−iωu~i)∗+τ~ikj(−iωu~i,k)∗−ρℓi2(−iωu~i)(−ω2u~i,j)∗].(70)

Integrating over the unit cell volume Ωcell, the total cycle-averaged energy transport vector ⟨𝑺⟩ satisfies the fundamental dynamic identity:

𝒗g=⟨𝑺⟩⟨W⟩+⟨T⟩,(71)

connecting the group velocity 𝒗g=∇𝒌ω directly to the volumetric energy flux and total stored energy. This identity was verified numerically in the internal verification suite (Test 5h) with an error below 10−8.
